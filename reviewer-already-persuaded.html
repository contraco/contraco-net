The Reviewer Was Already Persuaded

Most governance frameworks for AI place a human at the end of the work. The human reviews, signs off and carries the accountability. A randomised trial published in October 2026 tested what that sign-off is worth on decisions that have no single right answer. The result is uncomfortable for every organisation that relies on it.

The experiment

Fifty practitioners of causal inference analysed observational data for a fictional farming cooperative with the help of an AI coding agent, and delivered a recommendation to roll out or discontinue a programme. Four analytical decisions and the final recommendation each allowed more than one defensible answer. For every decision, a coin flip set which option the agent would lean towards. The agent was instructed to argue honestly and never to mislead. The participants did not know that a lean had been assigned. Eleven further practitioners completed the task without the agent and served only as a comparison group.

What happened

  • On the four analytical decisions, participants went with the agent's lean 86 percent of the time, against 50 percent by chance.
  • The direct effect of the lean on the final recommendation was smaller and not statistically significant under the registered estimator. In an exploratory analysis, however, the four analytical leans alone predicted 82 percent of the recommendations, against a baseline of 61 percent.
  • The agent voiced a side first on 77 percent of decisions. Where a participant had already taken a side and the agent then argued for the other, the participant switched 60 percent of the time, against 6 percent when the agent agreed.
  • Asked to justify their choices, 37 of 48 participants repeated the agent's arguments, sometimes almost word for word. Only 15 percent mentioned the agent at all, and more than half rated the recommendation as more their own than the agent's.
  • Participants who had followed the lean on all four decisions were as confident as the rest, at 71 against 73 on a scale to 100.
  • No participant characteristic, years of experience and expertise included, predicted deferral once the analysis corrected for testing several characteristics at once.

Why this matters for governance

The usual check on AI-assisted work compares the output against a correct answer. On these decisions there is none, and the output looks sound whichever option was chosen. The authors draw this conclusion themselves. Oversight of such decisions has to look at how they are made.

Our reading is that a human sign-off is not a verifier when the reviewer's judgment has already been shaped upstream. The signature remains. The independent judgment it is meant to certify does not.

The paper points to three consequences for an organisation:

  1. Name the decisions that need a person's own judgment, and ask for that judgment before the agent offers its view.
  2. Have the agent make the best case for the option it does not favour.
  3. Treat agreement between teams that use the same model as weaker evidence than independent agreement.

What the study does not show

  • The lean was deliberately amplified by a hidden instruction. Without it, the same agent made the same five choices in 91 percent of 123 simulated runs, so the instruction strengthened an existing behaviour rather than creating one.
  • It covers one task, one domain and one agent, and it reports average effects only.
  • The pre-analysis plan was registered after 17 participants had started. The paper is a company research paper and has not been peer reviewed.
  • Serious errors were far fewer with the agent, 3 of 50 against 6 of 11 without it, but the participants chose their group themselves, so that difference is not causal.
