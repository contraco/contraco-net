Prohibit, Disclaim, Authorise

Consumer AI agents now shop and book on a person's behalf. Sellers have started to answer in their terms of use, and the answers fall into three postures. Which one a seller chose says less about technology than about who pays when the agent gets it wrong.

The agent is already at the door

On 8 September 2026 Meta introduced Muse, a personal AI agent that can book travel, fill out forms and make purchases, rolling out in the United States. Meta describes a component it calls the Sentinel, through which nothing the agent does reaches the internet unless it is approved, and which asks the person for permission when needed. Payment runs through Link, built by Stripe, which generates a one-time-use card so that the real card details stay hidden. [Q1]

Agents like this reach sellers whose terms were written for people and for crawlers. Three published terms show three different answers to the same visitor.

Three postures, in the sellers' own terms

WalmartTerms for AI agents, newsroom post of 21 October 2026AuthorisationAgent purchases need the customer's approval by default, or stay within a spending limit the customer sets. Agents never receive card details, and the returns process applies even if an agent does not act as intended. [Q4]

Booking.com answered before the agents arrived. Its clause states plainly that "Booking.com does not authorize the use of automated means or automated assistants" to make bookings, and the same terms claim an entitlement to injunctive relief. [Q2]

Delta neither prohibits nor authorises. Its terms tolerate the agent, place its acts on the passenger, and say that such use "does not constitute Delta's authorization, approval, or endorsement". [Q3]

Walmart writes the contract the agent must satisfy. Agent Profiles, announced for the following month, give each agent its own profile that the customer links, limits and can delete, and agents that break the terms will be blocked from Walmart's site. [Q4]

The cheapest errors got the controls

Read side by side, the order runs against intuition. Walmart, whose errors are the cheapest to undo, is the only one of the three to build a mandate construct, with an approval default, a spending limit, revocation and separate attribution. It can afford to, because it already owns the remedy and says so in the same list of terms. Delta, whose error is a flown segment, and Booking.com, whose error can be a non-refundable rate, publish no mandate. One places the agent's acts on the passenger. The other refuses the agent.

The party with the cheapest errors built the control. The parties with the most expensive errors built the exit. That is our reading of the documents, not a statement by any of the three companies.

It also shows what none of them does. Walmart's controls govern permission, meaning how much an agent may spend and with whose approval. None of the three verifies correctness, meaning whether the item the agent chose is the item the shopper wanted. Walmart answers that question with returns, Delta by leaving the passenger to carry the agent's acts, and Booking.com by not letting the agent book at all. Why that check is still missing is the subject of Who Is Liable When an AI Agent Buys?

The fourth posture

The other European travel companies we read on 23 October 2026 have written no rule for an agent acting for a customer, as Confirmation Is Not Authorisation shows, and the only delegation construct we found in European travel was built in 2018 for a person.

What a seller needs is a fourth posture, admission on written terms. Which agents may enter, with what scope and spending limit, how the customer revokes that authority, and how the seller records it so that an auditor can read it afterwards. How that record fits into a governance model is set out in Agentic Commerce Governance, and the mandate chain diagnostic tests whether your own controls would carry it.
