Confirmation Is Not Authorisation

An AI agent can already pay for a trip in Europe. What it cannot yet carry is a written definition of what it is allowed to buy. We read the published terms of six European travel companies to see who has written that definition down.

Payment is not the blockade

On 2 July 2026 Visa announced live agentic transactions in Europe. AI agents browsed products, selected items and initiated purchases within parameters the consumer had set, and every payment was authenticated with a Visa Payment Passkey linked to a verified cardholder and an explicit instruction. More than thirty European issuers took part, four of them German, and lastminute.com was among the merchants. The background to that launch is in Why a Trip Was Among Europe's First Agentic Purchases.

The familiar objection, that strong customer authentication blocks agentic commerce in Europe, is therefore out of date. Strong customer authentication ties the confirmation of a payment to a verified person at the moment of payment, which is good design. European consumer law adds a separate requirement, that the consumer explicitly acknowledges the obligation to pay when the order is placed, as Why Your US Agentic Checkout Breaks in Europe explains.

Confirmation is not authorisation

Confirming one purchase is not the same as defining in advance what an agent may buy, for how much, from whom and until when. That second layer is the one that is missing. Agent payment protocols call it a mandate, as AP2 Mandate Explained describes.

The Visa launch also gave merchants a way to recognise verified agents, through its Trusted Agent Protocol and Agent Directory. Recognition answers which agent is at the door. It does not tell the seller what the customer has allowed that agent to do.

Protocol data shows the same gap. UCP Checker reads the Universal Commerce Protocol profiles of about 25,000 online stores. On 24 October 2026, 95 percent of valid profiles declared checkout and 56 percent declared a buyer consent capability. The capability that carries a user's mandate, the AP2 mandate, was declared by 0.2 percent. Authorisation controls are also being built outside that protocol, so the figure measures one mechanism rather than the whole market. It still shows where the work has gone first.

What six travel companies have written down

On 23 October 2026 we read the published website terms and, where available, the conditions of carriage of six European travel companies (Lufthansa Group, Air France KLM, Deutsche Bahn, Ryanair, TUI and Accor). None of them defines what an agent acting for a customer may do.

The automation clauses that do exist are rules against extracting data, and four of the five are limited to commercial purposes. A private agent booking one trip for one traveller is neither clearly permitted nor clearly prohibited by them. Deutsche Bahn is the exception. Its terms bar any access to content that does not go through the user interface, without reference to purpose, while expressly permitting lawful bookings. The line is drawn at the access path rather than at the purpose.

The conditions of carriage are silent. We searched the three airline conditions of carriage in full (Lufthansa, August 2026; KLM, April 2026; Air France, December 2024), and none contains a provision on automated or AI agents acting for a passenger. The only automated process Lufthansa mentions is its own automated check-in.

One European platform has written the agent into its terms, as a prohibition. Booking.com's customer terms, updated on 25 August 2025, bar access to its platform by automated means or automated assistants, AI-powered assistants included, whether or not the purpose is commercial, and state that Booking.com does not authorise automated assistants to make bookings on its platform. The same terms claim an entitlement to injunctive relief. How that prohibition compares with an American disclaimer and an American authorisation is set out in Prohibit, Disclaim, Authorise.

The exception was built for a person

One company has the construct, and it is instructive. Since September 2018 the Air France and KLM account terms have let an account holder delegate trip management to another account holder. The delegate may book and issue tickets, access bookings, manage trips and complete check-in. The delegate cannot change personal details and has no access to stored payment information. The account holder can cancel the delegation at any time.

That is, almost line for line, the Agent Profile that Walmart announced on 21 October 2026 for AI agents and says it will launch within a month. Built eight years earlier, for a person.

The perimeter and the wanted agent

Ryanair's terms of use declare ryanair.com the only website authorised to sell Ryanair Group flights, offer price comparison sites a written licence for the sole purpose of price comparison, and reserve an absolute right to act against unauthorised use, including with blocking technology. Its help centre adds that check-in must be completed by the passenger, by a passenger on the booking, or by an individual with personal knowledge of the passenger and of the contents of the luggage, citing the ICAO Technical Instructions and Regulation (EU) 2015/1998. Read together, these terms describe a perimeter that is defended deliberately. That is our reading of the documents, not a statement by the company.

It is a legitimate commercial position. The direct channel carries the ancillary offers that an agent booking the fare alone would skip, and no airline owes a scraper access.

A blanket blockade, however, answers only one of two questions. It stops the unwanted agent. It says nothing about how a seller is supposed to admit the wanted one, an agent carrying a real customer, a real budget and a real intention to buy, on terms the seller sets. To do that, a merchant has to be able to check the agent's scope, its spending limit and whether its authority is still valid, at the moment of the transaction. Nobody in European travel can do that today. The one platform that has written agents into its terms has written them out.

The European question

The European question is not whether an agent can pay. It is whether a traveller can set the boundaries of what an agent may buy, in a form the seller will honour and an auditor can read afterwards. In travel today the answer is no, and the component that would make it yes has been sitting in an airline account page since 2018. How that record fits into a governance model is set out in Agentic Commerce Governance, and where European payment law and the agent protocols meet is the subject of The Delegation Chain. The issuers' side of the question is in Trust Is the Bottleneck, and Banks Own It.
