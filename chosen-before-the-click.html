Chosen Before the Click

A user behaviour study published in October 2026 recorded 40 US shoppers, screen and voice, as they shopped inside two AI assistants across 224 tasks. It is not a study of agents that buy. Every choice in it was made by a person. Read as such, it is the clearest evidence so far of where an AI-assisted purchase is decided, and of how little of that decision a seller can see.

Two shelves, two decisions

In AI shopping a product competes three times. It has to earn the assistant's recommendation, hold up against the other product cards shown beside it, and then win as one seller's offer among others for the same product. The written answer frames the choice. The choice itself is made on the cards.

Product cards appeared in 220 of 224 tasks and carried the final product choice in 191, or 85.3 percent. In three categories where nobody was told to choose from a card, they still carried 87 of 104 choices. Product cards drew the first meaningful engagement in 165 of 219 tasks, against 47 for the written answer.

Where two or more product cards were shown, the first was chosen in 56 of 129 tasks, or 43.4 percent, against an equal-chance benchmark of 29.2 percent. At the offer cards, the first offer was chosen in 44 of 58 tasks with two or more offers, or 75.9 percent, against 37.8 percent. Position was not randomised, so these figures show where choices concentrated, not that position caused them.

The decision the analytics never see

Participants were asked to hover over whatever held their attention. In 79 of 218 tasks with usable click data, or 36.2 percent, shoppers hovered over product cards, clicked none of them and still reached a decision. Hovers exceeded clicks in every category and on both assistants. A referral report shows arrivals. It does not show the comparisons and rejections that happened before them or instead of them.

Our reading is that the party which pays for a wrong price or a missing image on a product card can see neither the card as the shopper saw it nor the evaluation that decided against it. Web analytics cannot close that gap.

Where the product data broke

The errors surfaced at the moment of commitment. Shoppers caught a card price that did not match the seller's page, a price attached to the wrong package size, two different prices for one product inside the same interface, one attribute stated two ways on the same page, and a sun protection rating rendered as a percentage. One shopper chose a product that had no useful image and disliked it the moment she saw it. The study records the mismatch as the shopper experienced it, not its cause, which could sit in a feed, a product page or the platform's own processing.

What shoppers said and what they did

Reviews were the primary reason for trusting a pick in 5 of 224 tasks, or 2.2 percent. Asked afterwards, 30 of 40 participants named reviews or testimonials among the most important factors. Specifications, brand familiarity and price together were the primary trust reason in 184 of 224 tasks, or 82.1 percent. The two measures have different bases and are a contrast rather than a paired result, but they are a reason to distrust an AI shopping strategy built on surveys alone.

What the study does not show

  • It measured selections, not purchases.
  • Every decision was made by a person inside an AI interface. It says nothing about agents that transact, about mandates or about payment.
  • Category, platform, task order and instructions varied together, so it is not a comparison of platforms.
  • Hovering was instructed, with a two-second threshold, and is not eye tracking.
  • Several tasks came from each participant, so its inferential results are exploratory.
  • Sessions were on desktop only, and the panel was experienced with AI, with 34 of 40 using chatbots at least three times a week.
  • All six categories are physical goods. There is no travel, no service and no booking in it, so nothing in it transfers to those markets except as inference.
  • It was commissioned by a company that sells the optimisation of product data for exactly these cards.

For a seller, the consequence is to check how its products appear on the cards of the assistants its customers use, and to measure presence, position and correctness there, not only the clicks that reach its own site. How the click is disappearing from reporting is set out in Dark Agentic Traffic.

Sources

  • User behaviour study of AI shopping, commissioned by a product data optimisation vendor and conducted by an independent research firm, 40 US participants, 224 tasks, fieldwork July to August 2026, published October 2026.
