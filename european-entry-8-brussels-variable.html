The Brussels Variable

contraco | September 2026 | 5 min read
Regulation EU Policy AI Act

Many US technology companies remember European regulation as an event. There was a GDPR project, a deadline, a round of contract updates and a new privacy notice, and then the topic went quiet. That memory shapes how they plan for Europe today, and it is the wrong model.

Regulation originating in Brussels is not a series of events. It is a permanent operating condition of the European market. New rules are adopted, phased in over years, amended, supplemented by guidance and standards, and transposed differently by different member states. A company selling technology into Germany is operating inside several of these cycles at once, whether it tracks them or not.

The AI Act shows how much the ground can move. It was adopted in 2024 as Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, with its obligations for high-risk AI systems due to apply from 2 August 2026. In 2026 the EU amended its own timetable. The Digital Omnibus on AI, Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, entered into force on 27 July 2026 and moved the high-risk obligations for stand-alone systems to 2 December 2027 and for AI embedded in regulated products to 2 August 2028. The transparency obligations of Article 50 kept their date of 2 August 2026. A company that planned only to the original deadline planned to a date that changed six days before it arrived.

The AI Act is only one layer. The Data Act has applied since 12 September 2025, with rules on access to data from connected products and on switching between cloud services. The NIS2 cybersecurity directive had to be transposed by member states by 17 October 2024, and transposition has been uneven. DORA has applied to financial entities since 17 January 2025 and reaches their technology providers through contracts and oversight. The Cyber Resilience Act has required manufacturers of products with digital elements to report actively exploited vulnerabilities and severe incidents since 11 September 2026, and its main requirements apply from 11 December 2027.

For a vendor, the commercial effect of these rules rarely arrives as a letter from a regulator. It arrives through customers. A German bank passes DORA requirements into its supplier contracts. A manufacturer asks for evidence of supply chain security under its national NIS2 rules. A procurement questionnaire adds a section on AI governance. Each of these changes what the vendor has to build, document, price and promise, and each lands in the sales cycle rather than in the legal department.

The same variable is also an opportunity. When a new rule starts to bite, buyers look for vendors who can show that they are ready. A company that can document its position early becomes the lower-risk choice, and in a market that buys on risk, being the lower-risk choice is a competitive position in its own right.

What most US companies lack is not legal advice. It is an owner. The Brussels variable needs a named function that maintains a regulatory horizon for each European market the company serves, connects that horizon to the product roadmap and the contract templates, and works with counsel in the EU rather than only with US counsel. Without that owner, each new rule is discovered by the sales team, one customer at a time.

The dates above reflect the legal position as of September 2026. They are exactly the kind of fact that should be checked again before any decision is based on them, which is the point of this part of the series. This is a commercial reading of the regulatory environment, not legal advice.

Who in your company owns the European regulatory horizon, and when did your product roadmap last change because of something decided in Brussels?
