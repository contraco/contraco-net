Localisation Is Not Translation

contraco | September 2026 | 5 min read
Localisation Messaging Value Proposition

Translating the website and the pitch deck into German is a necessary minimum. It is not localisation. A translation carries the words across. It leaves behind the commercial logic, the proof structure and the reasons to buy, all of which were built for a different reader.

Start with the tone of the claims. US B2B messaging leans on superlatives and bold outcome statements, and US readers discount them automatically. German B2B readers tend to take claims literally and to look for the evidence behind them. A page full of transformative, industry-leading and best-in-class does not read as confident. It reads as unsubstantiated. What German buyers look for instead is specificity about how the product works, what it integrates with, what it costs to run over its life, and what happens when something goes wrong.

Then look at the proof. The US proof stack is built from customer logos, short testimonials and growth metrics. In Germany the proof that moves a decision is a named reference in a comparable organisation, documented security and data processing, relevant certifications, and technical documentation in German. For cloud services, information security certification such as ISO/IEC 27001 and the attestation against the Federal Office for Information Security's C5 criteria catalogue are often part of the first qualification round, not late-stage paperwork.

The value framing changes as well. A US return-on-investment story is usually told in terms of speed and growth. A German buyer often frames the same decision in terms of risk reduction, reliability, total cost over a long operating life, and fit with established processes. The product can be identical. The reason to buy is different, and the material has to lead with the reason the buyer actually holds.

There are also legal minimums of presence that a translated US site often misses. A German website operated for business purposes must carry a provider identification, the Impressum, under Section 5 of the German Digital Services Act (Digitale-Dienste-Gesetz), which replaced the Telemedia Act in May 2024. Privacy information and cookie consent have to follow German and EU rules. A missing or outdated Impressum is a small thing technically, but to a German visitor it signals a company that has not yet really arrived.

Language itself matters in ways that machine translation does not catch. Formal address, consistent terminology across website, documentation and contracts, and technical precision all shape how competent a vendor appears. A German reader notices when the marketing copy uses one term, the documentation another and the contract a third, and draws conclusions about how the product itself is built.

Real localisation therefore starts from the German buyer's decision process rather than from the US asset library. Which questions does the buyer need answered, in which order, and with what evidence? Which documents will the procurement, legal and information security teams ask for? The website, the deck, the documentation and the contract are then built to answer those questions, in German, with the same care the US team put into the original.

That work costs more than translation. It also turns the German market from a place where the company is explaining itself into a place where it is being chosen.

If a German buyer read only your German website and documentation, could they find out where their data would be processed, who has run your product in production in their market, and what your contract says about liability?
