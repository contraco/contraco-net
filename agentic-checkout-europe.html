Why Your US Agentic Checkout Breaks in Europe

contraco | October 2026 | 4 min read
Agentic Commerce Consumer Law Market Entry

A US company that prepares its checkout for AI agents usually treats the job as engineering. The catalogue has to be machine-readable, the agent needs a payment credential it can use, and the order endpoint has to accept a final call without a person clicking through. In the United States that is most of the work. In Europe the last step is also a legal act, and a checkout that runs perfectly in Chicago can produce an order in Munich, Madrid or Milan that does not bind the customer at all.

The reason is one provision of European consumer law. Article 8(2) of the EU Consumer Rights Directive requires the trader to ensure that the consumer, when placing an online order that creates an obligation to pay, explicitly acknowledges that obligation. Where the order is placed with a button, the button may carry only the words “order with obligation to pay” or an equally unambiguous formulation. If the trader has not complied, the directive does not soften the consequence. The consumer is not bound by the contract or the order.

The rule applies in every Member State of the European Union, and it has applied to contracts concluded since June 2014. How strictly courts enforce it is contested. On 17 September 2026 the Kammergericht, the higher regional court in Berlin, ruled on an online car purchase completed through a button labelled only with the German word for “order”. The court held that no contract had come into existence, and not provisionally but finally. The buyer, who had been driving the car, is entitled to the purchase price back, less an amount for the use he had of it (Kammergericht Berlin, judgment of 17 September 2026, 1 U 25/25). Two days earlier, the higher regional court in Frankfurt am Main upheld a car purchase made through a button with the same label, because it found the ordering process transparent as a whole (Oberlandesgericht Frankfurt am Main, judgment of 15 September 2026, 9 U 76/25, not final). In July 2026 another senate of the Kammergericht refused to unwind an online purchase of an electric car (Kammergericht Berlin, judgment of 1 July 2026, 25 U 131/24). The Berlin court has allowed an appeal to the Federal Court of Justice. For a company launching in Europe this is not reassurance. If courts disagree about one word on a button, an order placed with no button at all is open territory.

For travel, the rule reaches further than most of the directive. The directive excludes passenger transport and package travel from nearly all of its provisions. Article 8(2) is one of the few it expressly keeps for both. The button rule therefore governs the flight ticket and the package holiday, which are exactly the purchases AI travel agents are being built to complete.

Now put an AI agent in front of that checkout. Nobody reads a button. The order is derived from a conversation and submitted through an interface that may have no button at all. The directive asks whether the consumer explicitly acknowledged the obligation to pay when placing the order. When an agent places it, who gave that acknowledgement, at what moment, and in what form can the trader prove it afterwards? As far as we are aware, no court has answered these questions for agents yet, and a checkout designed for the US market does not even ask them.

This is not a reason to keep agents out of European checkouts. It is a reason to design the order step for Europe before launch rather than after the first dispute. Three decisions belong in the European launch plan.

Treat the order step as a legal act. Decide how the customer's acknowledgement of the obligation to pay is captured when an agent orders, and use the same mechanism in every Member State rather than a market-by-market patch.

Record the mandate before the purchase. Keep a verifiable record of what the customer authorised the agent to buy, within which limits and for how long, made before the order is placed. Agent payment protocols call this record a mandate, as AP2 Mandate Explained describes. In a European dispute it is the document the order will be measured against. What European travel companies have and have not written down about such mandates is documented in Confirmation Is Not Authorisation.

Connect the acknowledgement to the payment. Make sure the confirmation the payment side relies on and the acknowledgement consumer law requires are not two unrelated events that a dispute can pull apart.

In the United States, the question for an agentic checkout is whether the agent can get through it. In Europe, the question is whether what comes out the other side is a contract. How the order step fits into a governance model for agent purchases is set out in Agentic Commerce Governance, and the wider legal environment a US company meets in Germany is the subject of GDPR Is Not Your Biggest Legal Risk in The European Entry Series.

This is a commercial reading of the legal environment, not legal advice. Every point above should be confirmed with counsel in the relevant Member State.

If an AI agent placed an order in your European checkout today, could you show who acknowledged the obligation to pay, and when?
