Agents Are Not Buying Yet

By the end of October 2026, personal AI agents were among the most discussed subjects on corporate earnings calls. What they had not yet produced was volume. Reporting from the same week shows where the market actually stands in three respects: what sellers see, what banks decided, and who carries the loss when an agent gets it wrong.

What sellers see

In conversations with reporters and on their own earnings calls, executives at roughly 50 companies in travel, internet services, food and self-storage played down any immediate disruption. A food delivery platform put the number of orders from agents in the hundreds. A hotel group called agent bookings insignificant. An online marketplace described the activity as very small, and a financial services provider as quite low.

Financial experts had suspected that users would ask agents to cancel unused subscriptions and memberships. A fitness chain reported that it had seen no cancellations by agents at all.

Where agents did arrive, they struggled with the ordinary mechanics of a purchase. A restaurant chain found agents failing on loyalty discounts and on orders placed after hours, and it is now cleaning up its website code so that agents can complete their orders. The launch of one consumer agent raised its agent-driven orders by a single-digit percentage.

What the banks decided

On 28 October 2026, several of Canada's largest banks confirmed that they keep personal AI agents out of online banking. Two block third-party personal agents from direct access to their authenticated services. One does not permit agents to access its digital properties or to execute transactions. One does not support agents opening or accessing accounts. One limits them while it monitors the concerns, and one is monitoring the development. The reasons given point to the protection of clients and their information. One chief executive framed it as a question of trust. If AI is to move into the broader economy, trust will matter as much as capability.

In the United States, banks moved the risk to the customer. A large US bank wrote to its customers on 2 October that anyone who gives an AI tool their username and password may be liable for the mistakes the tool makes. Another reminded customers that whoever shares their credentials remains responsible for the activity that follows.

Who pays when the agent buys wrong

The question beneath both reactions is unanswered. At an industry conference in October, the product lead for card issuing at a large payment processor said there is no case law on liability yet. A card network called a detailed audit trail of agent transactions essential. The chief executive of a bank-owned payments network reduced every dispute to two questions: did a human authorise the transaction, and what is the liability in either case.

Two companies have published consumer protection for purchases made by agents. A card network protects cardholders only when the agent is registered with it, and in a dispute the cardholder must submit a purchase intent document showing how the transaction departed from what was intended. A payment processor covers agent purchases made through its wallet in the United States, with extensive conditions on monitoring what the agent does. A bank chief executive called for a massive liability shift onto the technology companies that build the agents.

What this means for a seller

Our reading is that the volume is low and the rules that would let it grow are not yet written. Agents can already reach a checkout. What no party can show today is who authorised the agent, within which limits, and who is liable when it leaves them. A seller that settles these points in its own terms and systems before volume arrives will not have them settled for it in the first dispute.

  1. Which agents may transact on your site, and how do you recognise them?
  2. What record do you keep of what the customer authorised the agent to do?
  3. Who carries the loss when an agent buys outside that authorisation?

How sellers answer the agent today is set out in Prohibit, Disclaim, Authorise. Who checks the agent itself is the subject of Agentic Commerce Liability.
