AI Terms for Decision-Makers

The same AI terms appear in board papers, vendor presentations and strategy documents. Many decision-makers nod along, although nobody in the room means the same thing by them. Anyone who has to assess an offer, approve a budget or assign responsibility needs these terms in plain language. Here are the 25 that matter most, each with what it means and an example from everyday business.

By contraco | November 2026

The foundations

Large language model (LLM)

A language model trained on very large volumes of text that predicts which word is most likely to come next. The well-known AI assistants are built on such models. Think of an employee who has read every catalogue and every manual but has never been on site. The wording is confident, the checking is missing.

Token

The building blocks into which a language model breaks text. Common words are a single block, while rare or long words are split into several pieces that do not follow syllables. The model calculates in tokens, and usage is billed in tokens, much like a taxi meter.

Training

The learning phase in which a model takes on patterns from data before it is used. A model knows what happened after its training ended only if it is allowed to look it up. Answers on current prices or rules therefore need checking.

Context window

The amount of text a model can take into account at once in a conversation, meaning the question, attached documents and the conversation so far together. Anything beyond it drops out. In a very long conversation the model can lose what was agreed at the start.

Multimodal

A model that processes images, speech or video as well as text. It can be shown a photo of an invoice or given a voice note and will respond to it.

Open-weight model

A model whose trained weights are published, so that a company can run it on its own servers. This is not the same as open source, because training data and training code often remain unpublished. What matters most to a decision-maker is that the data can stay in house.

Working with AI

Prompt

The instruction or question given to the AI. Prompting means writing it in full sentences, with purpose, context and the result wanted. It works like a customer briefing, the more precisely the wishes are described, the better the offer.

Chatbot

A program that holds a conversation in sentences and answers questions. A chatbot gives information. Whether it can also act, for example book or order, depends on what it is connected to.

Copilot

An AI assistant that works inside office software such as email, word processing, spreadsheets and video calls. The name comes from a large software vendor and is now used generally for assistants of this kind. The picture is the co-driver, while the human keeps the wheel.

Deep research

A feature in which the AI researches a question on its own over several minutes, across many sources, and delivers a report with references. It does not replace checking, but it shortens the first survey of a topic considerably. Open the sources before passing on any figure.

RAG, retrieval-augmented generation

A method in which the AI looks up defined in-house documents, such as manuals, contracts or product data, before it answers, and answers on that basis. It lets a company put its own knowledge to work without retraining a model. Invented answers become rarer, but they are not ruled out.

Vibe coding

Creating software by describing in everyday language what it should do and letting the AI write the code. Business departments can build small tools themselves this way. Security, data protection and maintenance of those tools remain the company's responsibility.

Quality and risk

Hallucination

The AI invents something, a figure, a source, a quotation, and presents it with the same confidence as a correct answer. This is not a rare glitch but follows from how the model works, since it produces what is plausible, not what has been checked. Typical results are invented sources, wrong figures and quotations nobody ever said.

Bias

Systematic distortions that a model takes over from its training data. If past decisions were one-sided, the AI repeats them. This matters most in recruitment, lending and pricing.

Guardrails

Technical and organisational limits that define what an AI may and may not do, such as which topics it answers, which data it may access and above which amount it may not act without approval. Guardrails are set before deployment, not after the first incident.

Human in the loop

A person reviews or confirms before the AI's output takes effect. That is worth only as much as the reviewer's own judgment. Whoever reads the AI's proposal first is no longer independent on judgment calls, as The Reviewer Was Already Persuaded shows.

Prompt injection

Hidden instructions inside content the AI reads, such as a web page, an email or a document, that make it do something other than what it was asked. The more an AI is allowed to act on its own, the greater the risk.

Shadow AI

AI tools that employees use without the company's approval, often on private accounts. Company data leaves the house without anyone seeing it. Shadow AI often appears where the company offers no usable tool of its own.

Agents

AI agent

An AI that does not only answer but acts, meaning it searches, compares, fills in forms, books, pays or sends messages. The difference in one sentence, the assistant answers and the agent works. That raises the question of who instructed it, within which limits, and who is liable when it oversteps them.

Agentic commerce

Commerce in which an AI agent selects, orders and pays on a customer's behalf. For a seller this means the customer may never see its website, because the agent makes the choice. Who controls such purchases is the subject of the control layer of agentic commerce.

Agent register

A directory of all AI agents in a company, with purpose, responsible person, access rights and limits. Without one, nobody knows which agents act in the company's name.

Steering and cost

Inference

Each individual use of a trained model, meaning every question and every answer. Inference causes the running costs, usually billed by token. Anyone rolling AI out widely therefore plans for usage volume, not only for licences.

Fine-tuning

An existing model is trained further on the company's own examples, so that it handles a particular task, language or tone better. It takes more effort than RAG and pays off mainly when the form of the answer is to change, not only its knowledge.

GEO, generative engine optimization

The work of being named and recommended in the answers of AI assistants. Search engine optimisation aimed at a place in a list of results. An AI often names only two or three providers, and whoever is missing does not exist for that request.

EU AI Act

The European law on artificial intelligence. It sorts AI systems into four risk levels, from prohibited through high risk and transparency obligations to minimal risk. It has applied largely since August 2026, and for high-risk uses such as recruitment from December 2027.
