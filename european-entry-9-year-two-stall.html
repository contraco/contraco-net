Why European Expansion Stalls in Year Two

contraco | September 2026 | 5 min read
Scaling Year Two Retention

Year one of a European expansion usually looks better than it is. A founder or senior executive spends real time in the market. There are launch events, conference appearances and a handful of early customers who buy on vision and personal relationship. The board sees logos and momentum, and the expansion is declared on track.

Year two is where the foundation is tested. Most expansions that fail do not fail at launch. They stall quietly, somewhere around the eighteenth month, in a way that is hard to see on a dashboard until it is already expensive.

The first cause is attention. The senior people who opened doors in year one return to the priorities of the home market. The relationships they built were personal, and German buyers in particular notice when the person they trusted is no longer in the room. The local team inherits accounts that were won on someone else's credibility.

The second cause is the customer mix. Early adopters are not representative of the market. They bought because they were willing to take a risk on a new vendor. The next wave of buyers is not, and it asks for everything the early adopters waived, meaning references in its own segment, a contract reviewed against German law, documented data processing and support during its own working day. If those were never built, the pipeline fills with interested accounts that do not close.

The third cause is renewals. The first European customers come up for renewal in year two, and their decision depends on what happened after the sale. Was support available in their time zone and language? Did the product adapt to their processes? Did anyone from the vendor come back once the contract was signed? A first customer that does not renew is not one lost account. It is a lost reference, and in a reference-driven market that loss compounds.

The fourth cause is measurement. When headquarters compares the European business with US ramp curves, year two looks like underperformance even when it is normal for the market. The response is often pressure on the local team, a change of leadership or a cut in investment, each of which resets the relationships the market was just starting to trust.

The signals of a stall are recognisable. Deals stay in late stages for quarters. Every contract is negotiated from scratch. The same questions about data and liability come back in every account. Renewals are uncertain, and the local team spends more time reporting to headquarters than meeting customers.

The remedy is to design year two during year one. That means a reference programme that turns early customers into references in the segments the company wants next, a standard German contract position, support that matches the customer's working day, delegated authority for the local team, and success measures calibrated to European buying cycles rather than imported from the US. It also means an executive sponsor who stays visibly involved after the launch has been celebrated.

If your first European customers came up for renewal next quarter, would they renew, and would they agree to act as references for the segment you want to win next?
